## 1. Purpose and scope

This report exists to do four things and nothing else:

1. **File** the respondent's complete answer set as a durable, citable project record with correct
   provenance.
2. **Quantify** the record's evidentiary weight — where it is dense, where it is thin, where it is
   non-discriminating, and where it is anchored by the instrument's own wording.
3. **Surface** every internal tension, ambiguity, and open question, so that nothing is later mistaken
   for a settled finding.
4. **Prevent** the record from being laundered into design authority. The record is evidence about a
   different game, recalled by one person, at one point in time.

### 1.1 What this report explicitly does not do

- It does not convert any recollection into a TCIndustries rule, invariant, or constant.
- It does not propose mechanics, tuning, or numbers. Where a number appears, it is quoted as a recalled
  magnitude and is marked as such.
- It does not assess SWG as a design, nor rank it against TCIndustries.
- It does not change the status of any other document in the repository.
- It does not resolve tensions by assertion. Tensions found in v1.0 were put to the author as written
  questions (§8); where Addendum A resolves one, the resolution is the author's own statement, reproduced
  verbatim, and is not overwritten onto the original answer.

### F-10 — The Merchant profession's XP economy was capital-gated, and the record now holds three attested variants of "service XP" ( **NEW in v1.2** )

> **⚠ CORRECTED at v1.5 — read F-11 before relying on this finding.** The author has since corrected the
> underlying recollection **in their own words: "My memory is incorrect."** The standing yield was **50
> Merchant XP per active vendor-hour**, not a daily yield; it was paid for each **active (maintained)**
> vendor **regardless of sales**; and it carried two further components — interaction XP (capped once per
> 15 minutes across all the merchant's vendors) and structure-access-fee XP. The phrases "standing daily
> yield" and "capital-gated" used below are therefore **both superseded**: the yield was hourly, and the
> gate was a compound of an XP threshold (405,000 to Master Merchant), a **skill-unlocked vendor count**, and
> the maintenance cost of keeping each vendor active. The text below is **retained unamended as the v1.2
> record**, per GR-007.6 — corrections are marked in place, never silently applied. See **F-11** and **§8.2**.

Addendum B supplies the SWG-side account of the one service profession the 223-answer record covered
worst (R returned 5 one-word answers of 7), and it turns out to be the most mechanically specific account
in the whole file.

> "Merchants gain XP by simply selling things. Their worst skill tree is the Vendor skill tree because
> they get XP per day for each Vendor you have or something which severely gates the time it takes to
> become a Master Merchant."

**What this adds:**
1. **A third attested variant of service XP.** The record now holds three SWG-side service-XP shapes:
   - **Medic / Doctor** — **scaled with severity**: "It scaled in SWG which meant heavily wounded people
     were sought after eagerly by doctors." (Q91)
   - **Entertainer** — **XP per buff applied** (`SWG_PreCU_GDD.md` line 884; the respondent's own buff
     answers, Q82–Q85, F-7).
   - **Merchant** — **XP per sale**, plus a **standing daily yield per Vendor operated** in the Vendor
     tree.
2. **A standing yield is a different animal from a per-act award.** The Vendor tree paid "XP per day for
   each Vendor", so the Merchant's Vendor-tree XP was not earned by a service act at all — it accrued from
   **holding infrastructure**. This is a fourth distinct shape, and it is the one the respondent named as
   the worst part of the Merchant career.
3. **The gating was capital, not time.** "severely gates the time it takes to become a Master Merchant."
   More Vendor XP required **more Vendors**, each of which cost money to keep — so the progression rate of

   simultaneously a money sink (vendors must be maintained) and an XP engine. That is an unusually tight
   coupling: **one balance decision (vendor upkeep) simultaneously sets a money-sink strength, a
   progression rate, and a time-to-cap.**
4. **It explains a gap in the record.** The respondent's own reason for not recalling mission payouts is
   structural: "mission payouts I dont really remember **because I was a Master Merchant**. Merchants gain
   XP by simply selling things." The Respondent's XP came from selling, so mission payouts were not
   salient to them. See §7 item 5.

**Consequence (v1.2).** The record now contains a directly relevant historical observation on **both**
mechanisms that TCIndustries currently holds open: service-career viability (SERV-001, LOCKED, mechanics
TBD under SERV-002) and money-sink design (OQ-009, `proposals/TCIndustries_OQ-009_Currency_Money_Sinks_Proposal_Drafter_v1_2026-09-19.md`).
The observation is that a *capital-gated* progression curve can be produced by making a **standing
infrastructure yield** the XP source, and that the respondent regarded that mechanism as the worst part
of the career — "severely gates the time it takes to become a Master Merchant."

**This is evidence, not instruction.** It closes nothing. SERV-002's mechanics remain TBD, OQ-009 remains
open, and the project owns the balance decision. It is filed because a design question that the project

### F-14 — Respecialisation was free, unlimited, and did not refund experience — and the free respec created a paid retraining market.

> **Q10:** "Skill boxes could be surrendered "at any time" using the skill window, returning the skill
> points. No cost. Free."

> **Q12:** "No" — no per-day limit, no total limit, no restriction on which skills, no cooldown, no cost

**Assessor note on an internal tension.** Q10 says surrendering returned "the skill points"; Q11 says the

three (A economic cost, B retraining time, C capability decay / surrender).

**Bears on:** OQ-011 (named-open: economic cost / retraining time / capability decay). This is evidence
that a fourth class existed and functioned in practice, and a concrete historical instance of it. **It

> **Q22:** "They would duplicate credits so currency was counterfeit. Developers found the dupe, **mass

> **Q24:** "People like me were "billionaires" and hoarded money and resources. Why? **For fame** or just

**Assessor note.** This is a **historical instance of an exploit-driven inflation event and its
remediation**, for which the project's exploit-policy work (OQ-012 / SAFE-002) and currency work

**Bears on:** OQ-009, OQ-012 / SAFE-002. **Evidence of a historical outcome; not a ruling on the

**Amended at v1.6.** The author's refusal of the "machine's dice" phrase is recorded at **D-4** as an

### F-30 — The vendor fee could be driven to zero, and a vendor was at once free storage, an XP faucet and a sales channel. ( **NEW in v1.6** )

Addendum D, D-Q15:

> "a merchant could set the price of an item to anything, including 0 credits. My payments were 100 credits a
> day until I realized I could exploit the 0 credit cost item system and store thousands of items in a
> single vendor for free AND gain XP for it existing."

and D-Q02, on why the fee went unnoticed:

> "Vendor Maintenance fees were very poorly documented i.e. almost non-existent. This required trial and
> error pricing for vendor fees. If it is 1 credit per 100 credit items without fractional credits allowed
> (round up) then this was very difficult to test to be true."

**What this adds to F-17.** F-17 established that vendor fees were **bypassable** — "a sink instrument that
a player can set to zero by choosing a price is not a sink". Addendum D supplies the bypass as a
**first-person account of actually doing it**, and supplies the reason it went unnoticed: the fee was
**undocumented, quantized to whole credits with no fractions, and therefore empirically untestable at small
scale**. A sink instrument whose rate cannot be observed cannot be tuned, priced or policed.

**It also completes the vendor's economic role.** With D-Q02 and D-Q04, the vendor is now fully
characterised as a **skill-granted object** (F-17) that (a) **stores goods at zero marginal cost** when
priced at 0, (b) **pays a standing XP yield for existing** (F-11 — 50 XP per active vendor-hour, paid
regardless of sales), and (c) serves as a **sales channel** whose listed-price fee is a rounding artefact.
**In SWG the vendor's primary function was therefore not retail — it was a free, XP-paying warehouse.** The
author found this by trial and error; the game never documented it.

**Bears on:** OQ-009 (sink classes), OQ-016 (vendor and retail structure), SERV-002. **Closes nothing.**

### F-31 — The immortal item bug was a percent-damage-plus-round-up artefact, and it made the author's own crafted weapons rare and expensive. ( **NEW in v1.6** )

Addendum D, D-Q06:

> "1/1 bug was never fixed. people reported that stats changed but I never witnessed this happen to any of my
> items. 1/1 bug also included the immortal item bug which meant the item could never be destroyed because
> cloning decay caused percent damage and condition was always rounded up. It is possible that stats were
> affected, but none of my items ever had affected stats. **This made the weapons I sold incredibly rare and
> expensive.**"

**What this adds to F-16.** F-16 recorded the 1/1 bug as a bare fact — an item decaying to zero became a 1/1
item and could not be destroyed. Addendum D gives it a **mechanism** (cloning decay is *percent* damage;
condition rounds up, so it can never reach zero) and a **consequence that runs the opposite way** from the
one the bug appears to have. If items could not be destroyed, **destroyed crafted goods essentially did not
exist** — which made the author's own weapons rare and expensive, and therefore valuable.

This is a **market effect produced by a technical defect**: the scarcity was manufactured by the *inability*
to destroy, and the author stood on the benefiting side of it. It belongs beside F-20 (operating sink
classes) and F-30 (vendor economics). And it carries a design caution that does not depend on SWG: **a
rounding rule in a durability system silently becomes an economy rule in a crafting market.** A one-percent
condition floor is invisible to the player and absolute to the merchant.

**Bears on:** OQ-010 (durability, decay, repair) — where the option set does not contemplate a durability
rule with a market effect. **Closes nothing.**

### D-3 — Instrument defect in the v2 form: truncated question text and a dead export path (advisory, procedural)

Two defects were found in `proposals/SWG_Historical_Answers_v2.html` — the fill-and-export form for the v2
instrument — **before any v2 answer was collected**. Both were found by the respondent, not by the
assessment. They are recorded here because the v2 instrument is auditable only if its transport mechanism is
auditable with it.

**D-3a — truncated question text.** The generator that built the form from
`proposals/SWG_Historical_Accuracy_v2_Targeted_ReAsk.md` appended a question's wrapped continuation lines
only when they were indented by exactly four spaces. Eleven continuation lines in that instrument are
indented by three, so the tail of every affected question was silently dropped. **Seven of the 56 questions
were rendered short — Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 (Part A) and Q6, Q8, Q9 (Part B)** — and the worst case, Q1, lost 277
of its 377 characters, including the whole of its second and third sentences. Separately, the same parser
captured a category's framing note only when that note occupied a single line; **eleven of the thirteen
notes were dropped entirely** (only Parts K and L, the two single-line notes, survived). The instrument
markdown itself was never damaged — the defect was in the rendering step only.

**D-3b — dead export path.** The generator assembled the form's browser script inside a JavaScript template
literal. Inside a template literal an invalid escape such as `\*` collapses to `*`, so the browser's
markdown-stripping expression `/\*\*([^*]+)\*\*/g` was emitted as `/**([^*]+)**/g` — which JavaScript lexes
as a **block comment**, leaving a bare `g` identifier behind it. Every click of *Export answers for pasting*
therefore threw `ReferenceError: g is not defined` and produced no output. The defect was invisible to
static syntax checking, because the emitted code was syntactically valid and failed only at run time.

**Correction.** The form was rebuilt. The browser script now lives in a separate source file that is
injected verbatim, and the build asserts by SHA-256 that the injected script is byte-identical to its
source, so this failure mode cannot recur silently. Each question's plain-text stem is computed at build
time and stored in a `data-stem` attribute, so the browser performs no markdown stripping at all. The parser
now accepts continuation lines at any indentation width, and captures multi-line notes whole. All 56
rendered stems were verified character-for-character against the instrument markdown, and the export path
was then exercised in a live browser: 56 records emitted, 56 of each of the six template fields, no
truncated stems, no dropped fields, no runtime error.

**Effect on the record: none.** No v2 answer had been collected when the defects were found and corrected,
so no answer was ever given against a truncated stem and none was ever lost to the broken export. Recorded
for the audit trail only.

**Scope note — the v1 form and the v1 record are unaffected.** The same generator pattern was checked against
the v1 form: its export path read the rendered stem directly and never used a regular expression, so it did
not carry D-3b. All 223 v1 questions were verified character-for-character across three independent
sources — the instrument markdown, the v1 form as rendered, and the stems recorded in the raw export — with
**0 mismatches**. The raw export and the v1 record stand unaltered.

the only occurrence of the phrase in the entire record.

The respondent's answer was a correct rejection: "I dont know what 'Machines dice' means", followed at
Addendum D by a direct question — "Where is this term from? Is it from Star Wars Galaxies? What relevance
does it have?"

**This is D-1 recurring.** D-1 was raised at v1.2 to name the v1 instrument's failure: stems that imported
generic-MMO premises instead of SWG's actual systems. The v2 instrument was written explicitly to avoid
that, and its §0 states the rule: *"No stem may assert a mechanic that is not attested in SWG Pre-CU."* A
stem is not an assertion of a mechanic, but **a non-SWG premise inside a stem is the same failure**. The v2
instrument did not fully avoid it, and it did so in the one place the assessor had most reason to be careful
— a question about a system the respondent had personally used, and had just described in detail.

**The assessment's error was compounded.** At v1.5, §3.3.1 classified the respondent's "I dont know what
'Machines dice' means" as a hedge in the prose. That classification misread the answer and **counted the
respondent against a defect the respondent did not commit but the assessor did**. Corrected at v1.6.

**Effect on the record: none on the answer.** The substantive half of the C-Q33 answer stands: "Cant
control the results of experimentation. You can choose not to experiment, but that is wasting an
opportunity to improve your item." Only the comparative clause about how players talked about it was
unanswerable — and it was unanswerable because the assessor asked it wrongly. The record is unchanged; the
**question** is defective.

**Instrument-level lesson, stated for the audit trail.** An assessor writing a question for a domain the
respondent knows well is **more** likely, not less, to import an outside idiom without noticing, because
the surrounding content looks safe and the assumption "this one will be fine" does the work. The check that
catches it is **mechanical, not editorial**: search the instrument for every term that is **not attested in
the historical source** and **not attested in the respondent's own vocabulary**, and remove or justify each
one. That check was run for "Service XP" (F-9) and was **not** run for "machine's dice". It should be run
for every term in every instrument before it is put to a respondent.

## 5. Design-significant recollections

This section records what the respondent actually said, grouped by domain, with question references and
direct quotation. **Every quantity below is a recalled magnitude, not a measurement and not a tuning
target.** The section is descriptive: it does not recommend any of it for TCIndustries.

### 5.1 Combat, HAM, and the shape of a fight

The most complete and internally detailed domain in the record.

- **No cover, no line of sight.** "no cover no line of sight, it was ignored. as long as you targeted
  someone you could attack them." (Q10) In a 3D MMO. The respondent states this flatly.
- **No ammunition.** "no ammunition" (Q13) — effectively unlimited shots.
- **No facing or flanking.** "not used in this game" (Q4).
- **No reach differentiation.** "no. everyone fought at the same distance. attack skills such as change
  posture and knockdown would stop people from moving." (Q3)
- **Melee was auto-attack plus specials.** "auto attack with specials. In fact you usually spammed
  specials. no stances no combos other than spam specials in certain order i.e. dizzy then knockdown"
  (Q5). The only "combo" was ordering two independent specials: dizzy causes an action to fail,
  knockdown prevents acting until getting up (Q16).
- **Specials had no cooldowns; they were gated by activation time and HAM cost.** "no cooldowns but they
  had activation times to prevent unlimited spam and most would be the same speed as the weapon used.
  They had HAM costs so players could accidentally incap themselves if they used specials too many
  times. **HAM costs were the gate for high level combat.**" (Q15)
- **Specials were queued, and the queue was a mechanic in its own right.** "hotbar, terminal commands,
  macros. Actions were queued. queues could be cleared. actions which became invalid were skipped."
  (Q14). **Addendum A resolves the count (Q-C4):** "PvP 1v1 will be around **12 special attacks** as
  players would run out of HAM to use more. High level players would spam specials non stop due to buffs
  and healing." The original "two dozen specials / 50 or more" (Q14) is therefore an **availability**
  count, not a cast count — and the cast count is bounded by HAM, exactly as Q15 says.
- **Defensive specials were passive and automatic.** "no these skills were passive and automatic" (Q18).
- **Specials were not felt to be decisive.** "no. You basically got hit until you died. the magnitude of
  damage was not conveyed any differently from normal except for criticals which would say critical hit."
  (Q17)
- **Crowd control was rare and unreliable.** "very few classes had crowd control and they failed to work
  most of the time. was very rare to see them used and working." (Q19) — and yet the Teras Kasi Artist
  dizzy→knockdown combination was the one universally feared encounter: "Everyone was afraid of the Teras
  Kasi Artist Dizzy Knockdown where you would spend 5 minutes being unable to do anything." (Q98)
- **HAM pools were distinct, and MIND was the death pool because it could not be healed.** "they were
  distinct but everyone watched MIND because MIND could not be healed. Rifleman abilities all attacked
  MIND pool which made them the most deadly." (Q20, Q21)
- **Time-to-kill was long.** "low levels was about 10 minutes. high levels fully equipped was also about
  10 minutes but usually 30 minutes due to healing if they could heal themselves. low levels generally
  didnt heal themselves because they were noobs." (Q6); ranged "10 to 20 minutes" (Q12). **Addendum A
  gives the model explicitly (Q-C3): 1v1 is "~10 minutes… a normal but long fight and that would not
  include healing"; 30–60 seconds applies to **group PvP** — "multiple people against one person or
  focus fire or unfair matchups."** The author also notes the ratio that makes the two coexist: buffs
  lasted "like 4 hours… maybe 2 hours for normal buffs", so "Preparing for so long to fight for 30s is a
  ridiculous ask." (See F-5.)
- **Buffs, not gear, were the primary combat lever.** "Buffs were mandatory for high level players. Buffs
  increased HAM by thousands or tens of thousands (it is hard to remember) depending on the ability of
  the doctor's medicines. Unbuffed players in PVE or PVP would usually die immediately reducing the time
  to kill from 10 - 20 minutes to maybe 1 - 5 minutes. Or more often one hit kill." (Q23). **Addendum A
  quantifies it (Q-C5):** "You definitely got buffed for at least **1000 extra HAM**… the very high level
  doctors with high level medicines could buff you for **10000 - 12000 HAM**." Critically, those top-tier
  buffs "were only for player association guildmates as I dont think you could pay a doctor to give you
  that level of buff." — see **F-7**.
- **Range bands existed and changed to-hit.** "yes there were range bands. range bands gave bonuses or
  negatives to hit depending on which weapon you would use. rifleman would try to keep their distance.
  most players tried to fight within 30m of each other unless melee." (Q9)
- **Move-and-shoot was allowed, at a penalty.** "you can move and shoot but it included a to hit penalty."
  (Q8)
- **Incapacitation was a 30-second self-recovery, with a death threshold and an uncounterable finisher.**
  "Incap lasted for 30 seconds. You would get up on your own after 30 seconds. allies could help you.
  being incaped 3 times within a short time limit would kill you. being /deathblow while incap would
  kill you and allies could not stop this action from happening." (Q25) "no bleed out timer only incap
  timer" (Q26); "PVE would always /deathblow if they are hostile and not a neutral" (Q27). Reviving
  placed you too far away to help your group, but solo players used it as free travel (Q28).
- **Wounds were a minor inconvenience.** "minor inconvenience" (Q24); wound treatment was rare and
  "felt like a formality" (Q79), because wounds rarely got bad enough to need it (Q94).

### 5.2 Progression and skills

- **No XP debt, no XP loss on death.** "no" (Q30).
- **Skill unlearning was possible** — a genuinely uncommon MMO affordance. "players can choose to
  unlearn skills by clicking the remove skill option in the skill tree. players may want to do this to
  explore other careers or to temporarily free up skill points to take a temporarily needed skill, like
  medicine or heal." (Q33)
- **Skill trees were deep and genuinely diverse.** "very deep and branching" (Q35); "Genuine build
  diversity. No must have boxes." (Q36); hybrid builds were "rare but very viable" and were the mark of
  the best players (Q37).
- **The skill-point cap degraded in felt-generosity over time** as the game accumulated unusable skills
  (Q38).
- **Skills inflated within a tree.** "higher skills like /headshot3 were far superior to /headshot2 or
  /headshot1 so lower level skills were never used once the higher ones were attained." (Q39) The
  respondent's phrasing is careful and, unusually, technically exact: there were "no 'dead' skills but
  there were many skills which did not work."
- **Whole trees were broken.** "the entire Squad Commander skill tree skills never worked properly and
  most of the Commando skills didnt work either." (Q39); "Yes for some but some were completely broken and
  never worked correctly and were not worth trying." (Q199). **Addendum A explains *why* each failed, and
  the causes are different in each case (see the dedicated bullets below).**
- **Certification was a meaningful gate**, with one named exception. "people were always happy when they
  gained the ability to use Ubese armour over the rubbish Bone Armour. Weapon certification was
  meaningful too except for Pistoleers as they got the best gun almost straight away making the later
  guns pointless." (Q40); no lock-out felt beyond possibly Slicing from Smugglers (Q41).
- **Some skills were grindy by design**, specifically waiting-based ones: "notably the camping line where
  you had to wait for actual time to pass to gain experience or bounty hunter exploration where you had to
  travel" (Q29).

**Addendum A — progression pace was path-dependent, and the record now explains why.** This is the
single largest addition to §5.2 and it converts Q34's bare duration figure into a mechanism.

- **The two elite-duration figures measure different conditions.** "If you were given help then Elite
  careers could be mastered within a day of constant play. However, if you started the game from scratch
  with no knowledge and no help it would easily take you ten times that amount of time. So **240 hours**
  would be an accurate measurement." (Q-C1) — so **~24h assisted** (Q34) and **~240h unassisted**
  (Q200's stem figure) are both correct.
- **The Bounty Hunter grind had a concrete arithmetic.** The Exploration tree paid "1000 xp per mission"
  and needed "something like **300,000 or 500000 xp** to max out the tree", with "each mission took
  **30 minutes on foot**". "You could complete multiple missions in the same place if you were lucky. It
  was faster when mounts and vehicles were released." (Q-C1) This is the concrete form of Q29's "bounty
  hunter exploration where you had to travel", and it independently corroborates Q197 — mounts and
  vehicles did not only make travel faster, they removed a specific grind.
- **Squad Commander was structurally unplayable, not merely buggy.** "Squad Commander needed a group to
  use group skills to give commands and get XP, but their skills never worked and their combat skills were
  poor meaning they were not a viable class to play and **their class was never fixed to be playable**."
  (Q-C1) A group-dependent class whose group skills did not work and whose solo skills were weak, and
  which was never repaired.
- **Commando's heavy-weapon tree fought itself.** "their weapons fired slowly and missed a lot and had
  high HAM costs" — so the same HAM economy that gates combat (Q15) also gates the XP gain that is
  supposed to unlock it. (Q-C1) A progression system whose *cost* resource starves its own *reward*
  loop.
- **Two mastery paths were simply bad.** "Master Carbineer and Master Pikeman had terrible weapons,
  terrible accuracy, terrible damage which made leveling up unfun and slow." (Q-C1) A third named class,
  **Smuggler**, had "slicing tree… horribly slow to level up" (Q-C1) — notable because the Smuggler
  class itself was reported as one of the most valued (Q87).
- **The general principle, stated by the author:** grind length in SWG was a function of **tree design,
  weapon economy, group dependency, and travel time**, each of which could independently produce a
  punishing path. The 240h aggregate is the sum of those, not the story.

### 5.3 Economy — market structure, market access, and the mission-income anchor

*(v1.1: this heading was "the strongest single mechanism in the record". It was downgraded after Q-C8 —
see the first bullet below, which is the corrected reading of Q43.)*

The respondent's economic account is the most analytically valuable material in the file, and it
describes a system that did **not** behave the way the questionnaire's category title implies.

- **Prices were pinned to mission income — and that anchor is corroborated, but the "time-to-earn" reading
  of it is the author's own model, not an observation (v1.1 corrected).** "prices didnt rise because the
  base price was pinned to mission income which was 800 - 1000 credits. players could then calculate the
  amount of time it would take to earn something instead of using the credits as the main factor, **time
  to earn became the main factor**." (Q43) **Addendum A (Q-C8) corrects the reading of this answer:**
  "**The economy didnt behave like that**, but if earning money is based on how long you can play the game
  for then time to earn becomes the most important economic factor for player decision making." Addendum A
  also supplies the payout basis directly: "Mission payouts were **800 for something like a delivery
  mission and 1000 for a crafting one**. They would scale though with a **maximum payout of 10000** I
  think."

  **Consequence:** the *anchor* survives and is independently corroborated — mission payouts of 800
  (delivery) and 1,000 (crafting) match the "800 - 1000 credits" figure quoted in Q43, and both come from
  the same author. The *conclusion* drawn from it does not. In v1.0 this was reported as "the strongest
  single mechanism in the record"; **that is withdrawn.** The author states plainly that the economy did
  not behave that way, and that the time-to-earn framing is a reasoning model they apply, not a property
  they observed. **Cite the 800/1,000/10,000 payout band; do not cite "time to earn became the main
  factor" as a description of how the economy functioned.**

  **New limitation, stated by the author:** "It has been 22 years and it is hard to remember so far back
  especially since all records of the game from the official forums have been purged from online."
  (Q-C8)
- **There were no effective money sinks.** "Unfortunatley not. The only major money sink was housing and
  buildings but a player would choose to make and live in those. things like harvesters or factories were
  money sinks but were not very effective at their role as they made far more income than they consumed.
  travel and training costs were minimal." (Q44)
- **Players hoarded; merchants acted as the sinks.** "in theory the game was inflationary, but in reality
  players acted as money sinks (merchants especially) and would hoard money" (Q43).
- **Crafting income was skill-scaled, not character-scaled.** "crafting income was variable and based on
  the player ability, not character ability. once a characters skills were high enough they could earn a
  lot as an artisan or crafter." (Q48)
- **The bazaar was a real market specifically because of the price limit.** "Real market due to the price
  limit. Surprising what people would actually buy" (Q52).
- **The market had a two-tier access structure** — and the respondent, a Master Merchant, describes the
  lower tier as the problem. "Merchants didnt pay any type of merchant fees and could have vendors to put
  their stock in. They were an underutilized and undervalued class. I was a Master Merchant. It was fun.
  **Regular traders could only use the bazaar and were capped at price, amount of items they could sell,
  and were charged fees.**" (Q89) — echoed in the negative results (§6) as the bazaar's "nonsense."
- **Price stability had a documented cause and an end date.** "Stable. Until holocrons were released
  prices were always stable." (Q51); undercutting was tolerated because quality varied and because
  "upset sellers would simply buy the cheap stock and resell it" (Q53); cartels were "rare and short
  lived as competition was fierce on my server" (Q54).
- **The fastest money was boring, which is itself a design observation.** "The fastest way to get rich was
  prospecting / surveying and harvesting. You could harvest a lot of material and sell them for very high
  prices. This took a lot of time and most players considered it too boring to do." (Q50)
- **Tithing was social, not economic.** "made you feel part of the group or city." (Q46)

### 5.4 Crafting, resources, and the persistent world

- **Crafting was the systems high point and the respondent's own answer for "best-feeling system"
  (Q217).** "SWG crafting is legendary for being the best crafting system. it was fun, inventive, and
  engaging. it wasnt mini game choices and, in fact, could be programmed into a macro if you were good at
  programming. This allowed crafters to craft on the move or anywhere." (Q55). Assembly specifically: "the
  most fun experience for a crafter" (Q57).
- **Experimentation was luck, with a small skill margin.** "Essentially it was luck. there was a skill for
  experimentation but it was luck based. depending on the item it may give a 10% boost if you got an
  amazing success." (Q56). Quality mattered at the top end: "High and very High level items could make
  or break a sale an Amazing Success during crafting." (Q63).
- **Chasing perfect rolls was not toxic.** "Perfect rolls were very fun to chase and not toxic. Anyone
  could do it and the results were roughly equal across all players." (Q64)
- **Failure was a non-event with a training affordance.** "acceptable. In fact, one mode was practice mode
  where no item was produced and you got bonus experience. failed crafts were simply trashed with the
  trash command." (Q59)
- **Schematic choice was a design intent that the release build removed.** "The original rules of SWG had
  the idea that you would choose schematics, but the release version formally set the schematics. the most
  sought after schematics tended to be middle tier." (Q60, Q61)
- **Factories traded variance for volume.** "They were slower than making things by hand. The biggest
  benefit was stackable crates of items and no variability between items." (Q67); material change forced
  new schematics and new sources (Q66); they were expensive and not spammed (Q68).
- **Resource shifts created and destroyed economies and cities.** "Yes! Entire economies and player cities
  would come and go following a resource shift." (Q71); "Whole economies were created around specific
  material spawns." (Q73). Resources never depleted — "They just disappeared." (Q74) — and scarcity drove
  hoarding (Q75, Q76).
- **There was no gear in creature loot at all, and what did drop was worth ~20 credits.** "There were no
  loot npc items. SWG release only had junk loot that didnt do anything." (Q65); "Looted weapons were not a
  thing in SWG Pre-CU" (Q172). **Addendum A quantifies the junk:** "loot was junk and you sold it to the
  junk merchant for about **20 credits** - not worth picking up or carrying around." (Q-C6) Crafting was
  therefore the *only* route to a usable item, which sharpens Q217's survey→harvest→craft→persists loop
  from a description into a closed economic loop. See F-6.
- **The best-feeling-system answer is a full loop, and it is the most quotable line in the record:**
  "Crafting. Because it really was you making something. You surveyed the materials. You harvested the
  materials. You use the materials to make something useful and that thing persists in the world." (Q217)

### 5.5 Professions and the service economy

- **Healing decided fights.** "absolutely yes. healing meant the difference between winning and losing."
  (Q78) Medics were "critical to all combat classes and were highly valued" (Q80).
- **Stimpacks were not a crutch** — higher tiers required skill, and combat classes would not spend
  points on medicine (Q81).
- **The buff cycle was a routine, not a tax.** Buffs lasted "4 hours or so" and "would become routine for
  casual players"; musicians and entertainers did not buff in SWG's early days (Q84). Watching entertainers
  was social, not extractive: "the only people watching just came back from a big adventure and it was fun
  to tell people about what they did" (Q83). **MIND buffs were critical**, because MIND cannot be healed
  (Q85). **Addendum A gives two tiers of duration:** "buffs lasted for like **4 hours** or something, maybe
  **2 hours for normal buffs**" (Q-C3).
- **The top tier of the buff economy was a social privilege, not a purchasable service (F-7).** Addendum A:
  "You definitely got buffed for at least **1000 extra HAM**. I believe the very high level doctors with
  high level medicines could buff you for **10000 - 12000 HAM**. **Those kinds of buffs were only for
  player association guildmates as I dont think you could pay a doctor to give you that level of buff.** I
  was a Master Doctor Master Rifleman Master Merchant Combat Medic and would buff myself for that much
  for free." (Q-C5) The premium tier was bound to the relationship, and was given rather than sold — and
  the respondent occupied both sides of that structure, describing the market's two-tier access rules
  (Q89) while being on the privileged side of the buff economy.
- **Smugglers were among the most valued professions** — for slicing (making weapons and items more
  powerful), for drugs, and for combat versatility (Q87).
- **Scouts and Rangers had no combat role but a real economic one**: increased harvesting ability made
  crafters depend on them, "especially Chefs" (Q88).
- **Image design mattered to the alien races, less to humans** (Q86).
- **Service XP scaled with severity**, which produced a market for the wounded: "It scaled in SWG which
  meant heavily wounded people were sought after eagerly by doctors. Maybe it should be flat with extra xp
  scaled." (Q91)
- **The Merchant profession's XP economy was capital-gated, and the record now holds three attested
  variants of "service XP" (F-10).** "Merchants gain XP by simply selling things. Their worst skill tree
  is the Vendor skill tree because they get XP per day for each Vendor you have or something which
  severely gates the time it takes to become a Master Merchant." (Addendum B) The Vendor tree paid a
  **standing daily yield per Vendor operated** — not a per-act award — so the Merchant's best tree
  accumulated XP from **holding infrastructure** rather than from performing a service. The gating was
  **capital, not time**: more Vendor XP required more Vendors, each costing money to keep, so one
  balance decision (vendor upkeep) simultaneously set a money-sink strength, a progression rate, and a
  time-to-cap. Read against the two-tier market access at Q89, the Merchant career is the clearest case in
  the record of **capital itself being a progression gate**. See F-10 and §8.2.

### 5.6 World, travel, and the client

- **The world read as a world.** "World" (Q216) — the direct answer to whether it felt like a world or a
  game.
- **Emergent play was the real game and has never been replicated.** "Yes. That emergent play has never
  been replicated." (Q220); the sandbox promise was delivered (Q221).
- **Vehicles and mounts were the single largest change to exploration.** "Original SWG release didnt have
  vehicles and mounts. When they appeared game exploration exploded and everyone went fast everywhere it
  was great." (Q197)
- **Shuttles did not undermine exploration.** "Fast travel were shuttles and they did not undermine
  exploration." (Q210); shuttle schedules were "Immersive" (Q195).
- **Day/night and weather were immersion-only, explicitly not gameplay.** "Gameplay? No. Immersion? Yes"
  (Q207, Q208) — the respondent answered in exactly that form, unprompted, for both.
- **The client was held in unusually strong regard.** "Radial menu is one of the greatest inventions ever"
  (Q211); "Wonderful and amazing and still the best UI" (Q212); the UI did not get in the way of
  immersion (Q215).
- **Death cost nothing material.** "There was no item loss in SWG" (Q165); no insurance (Q166), no corpse
  run (Q167), no binding (Q180). Respawn was "Smooth. people would use it for fast travel." (Q168), and
  "You did not lose progress on death" (Q169).
- **NPCs and creature AI were alive** (Q204, Q205).
